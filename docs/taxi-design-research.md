# Arcade Taxi Design Language Research

_Prepared 2026-07-13 for an original, legally distinct game vehicle. This is a design-principles study, not a replication guide._

## Evidence method

- **Observed** means visible in a linked image/manual or explicitly stated by its publisher.
- **Inferred** means a design conclusion drawn from those observations.
- Priority is first-party Sega material and publisher-authored manuals. Where an old manual is no longer hosted by Sega, the report labels the preservation mirror rather than treating the host as the author. No surviving production concept sheets were found in this limited pass; official brochures, manuals, screenshots, and promotional wallpaper art are used instead.

## Executive finding

Crazy Taxi's durable visual formula is not one specific car. It is **an individualized character car plus a common taxi recognition kit plus exaggerated motion-readiness**. Across the series, 1950s and 1960s cruisers, muscle cars, tuner-like shapes, and a prewar hot rod can all belong to the same fleet. Saturated yellow, a compact TAXI marker, simple high-contrast graphics, an exposed driver/passenger area, and a wide, ready-to-jump stance make the family cohere.

The safest evolution is therefore to preserve the grammar - instant taxi readability, one bold silhouette gesture, visible personality, and arcade exaggeration - while replacing the specific body archetype, panel shapes, sign silhouette, graphic motifs, typography, and character-linked details.

## Series observations

### Crazy Taxi - arcade original (1999)

**Observed.** Sega's arcade-history page dates the game to February 1999 and supplies an official cabinet photo, gameplay screen, and brochure. The hero cab is a high-chroma yellow/orange open cruiser with a long hood and deck, low beltline, broad rectangular tail, chrome impact surfaces, simple lamp clusters, and dark/checkered accents. Its open cabin keeps the driver and fare inside the gameplay silhouette. Sega's later Dreamcast page explicitly describes four selectable open taxis; the publisher-distributed manual calls Axel's car a 1960s classic and Gus's car a junked 1955 model. [Sega arcade history](https://www.sega.jp/history/arcade/product/8973/) [Sega Dreamcast retrospective](https://dc.sega.jp/game_taxi.html) [Sega manual distributed through Steam](https://shared.akamai.steamstatic.com/store_item_assets/steam/apps/71230/manuals/DC_CT_PC_MG_EFIGS_FINAL_lr.pdf?t=1733765150)

**Inferred.** The recognizable unit is "wild personal cruiser converted into a taxi," not a standardized fleet sedan. Long horizontal masses communicate speed; the open cabin makes character performance readable; a few large trim/graphic bands survive the low camera and fast travel better than fine surface detail.

### Crazy Taxi 2 (2001)

**Observed.** The Sega-authored Dreamcast manual presents Slash, Iceman, Cinnamon, and Hot-D beside small front three-quarter vehicle drawings. The lineup ranges from low open cruisers and squarer late-century forms to Hot-D's upright prewar hot-rod shape with separated fender volumes. The manual says Iceman simply painted his existing car yellow, while Hot-D painted a favorite car after seeing West Coast crazy driving. In other words, taxi identity is an applied layer over different personal vehicles. The manual's own art direction uses yellow fields, red display type, flames, halftones, and hard-edged color blocks. [Crazy Taxi 2 manual - Sega-authored primary artifact, preservation mirror](https://www.digitpress.com/library/manuals/dreamcast/crazy_taxi_2.pdf)

**Inferred.** A successful fleet can span eras and classes if a shared kit controls color, sign language, and graphics. The character-car premise is a strong modular rule: begin with a distinct archetype, then add taxi components, rather than starting every variant from a generic cab.

### Crazy Taxi 3 / High Roller (2002-2003)

**Observed.** Sega's official High Roller page and brochure describe three themed courses and four drivers per course, for twelve drivers total. Its official gameplay image shows a more angular yellow open car than the original: broad flat rear deck, black louvers, twin round lamps, exposed chrome/metal bumper and exhaust elements, a compact TAXI topper, and a planted rear track. The Xbox manual ties vehicle behavior to personality - notably Zax's self-maintained car and renowned hydraulic technique - while retaining the 1960 cruiser, 1955 jalopy, and Crazy Taxi 2 roster across its course groups. [Sega High Roller arcade history](https://www.sega.jp/history/arcade/product/18121/) [Crazy Taxi 3 Xbox manual - Sega-authored primary artifact, preservation mirror](https://datassette.org/manuais/us-estados-unidos-jogos-xbox-classico-microsoft-manuais/crazy-taxi-3-high-roller)

**Inferred.** The third game pushes the family toward sharper rear graphics, exposed performance hardware, and stance as characterization. Suspension height, wheel fitment, deck treatment, exhaust, and light cluster are therefore high-value variant modules, not merely technical details.

### Crazy Taxi: Fare Wars / Double Punch presentation (2007-2008)

**Observed.** PlayStation's first-party release description identifies Fare Wars as a collection of Crazy Taxi and Crazy Taxi 2 with local multiplayer and soundtrack features, rather than a new vehicle generation. Sega's own promotional wallpaper gallery for the Japanese Double Punch presentation shows the drivers posed with their established vehicles in unified yellow-and-black line art: long classic convertibles, blockier cruisers, lower sporty forms, and Hot-D's upright hot rod all receive similar roof signage and graphic treatment. [PlayStation Blog product description](https://blog.playstation.com/2009/10/01/sega-classics-hit-psn-today-with-launch-of-pspgo/) [Sega official promotional wallpaper gallery](https://anan.sega.jp/anan_net_item/wallpaper_sega.html)

**Inferred.** Fare Wars is useful evidence for brand portability, not for a new base silhouette. Consistent rendering, livery, signage, and driver presentation can make a mixed-era roster look like one product family without forcing every car onto one shell.

### Adjacent arcade taxi designs

**Smashing Drive (Gaelco, 2000). Observed.** Gaelco's official sales flyer depicts a tall, chunky yellow super-cab whose state is changed by conspicuous attachments: enlarged wheels, roof hardware, side protection, and rocket-like thrust. Every power-up changes the outline enough to read at arcade speed. [Gaelco sales flyer - primary artifact at the International Arcade Museum](https://flyers.arcade-museum.com/videogames/show/1572)

**Taxi Chaos (Team6/Lion Castle). Observed.** The publisher describes seven unlockable taxis spanning muscle, exotic, Japanese tuner, and 1960s American classic archetypes, each with different attributes, while screenshots keep saturated color and taxi markers consistent. [Official publisher page](https://gs2games.com/taxi-chaos)

**Inferred.** The useful adjacent lesson is functional silhouette change. Accessories should not be tiny collectibles: a heavy bumper, wheel/fender package, roof unit, or boost module should visibly alter the car's read and imply its handling role.

## Cross-title design-principles matrix

| Attribute | Directly observed pattern | Principle for a new, distinct taxi |
|---|---|---|
| Silhouette | Mixed personal-car archetypes, often open-top, unified as taxis | Give the new car one dominant gesture of its own. A short-overhang targa fastback or compact rally-wagon profile would separate it from the series' long 1950s/1960s cruisers. |
| Proportions | Long horizontal masses, broad shoulders, generous passenger opening | Exaggerate one ratio for distance readability, but do not reproduce Axel's long hood/deck relationship or Gus/Hot-D's period-car profiles. |
| Stance | Wide, planted, visibly capable of hops, drifts, and impacts; hydraulics are character language | Treat ride height, track width, camber, and suspension travel as authored states. Keep wheels near the outer body envelope and leave room for readable compression/extension. |
| Wheel size | Wheels are prominent and separated clearly from the body, but usually retain chunky arcade tire sidewalls | Use mildly oversized wheels rather than thin realistic commuter tires. Make wheel diameter, tire profile, hub, and fender cap one coordinated swap set. |
| Body panels | Large simple planes with a few strong louvers, grilles, lamp shapes, bumpers, and exhausts | Spend geometry on the outer contour and 2-3 signature panel events. Put secondary seams and fasteners in normals/decals for game efficiency. |
| Color blocking | Yellow/orange dominates; black, white, chrome, and occasional red provide high contrast | Keep a high-chroma taxi color, but establish an original secondary palette and layout. For example, acid yellow plus petrol blue and warm white in an offset diagonal field. |
| Roof signage | Compact TAXI signs recur, but open cars sometimes make the sign visually subordinate | Build a unique sign system with two or three redundant cues: roof mast/bridge, illuminated rear meter bar, and door route badge. Do not rely on one copied rectangular topper. |
| Decals | Checkers, plates, sparse markings, and punk/flame graphic energy read more strongly than fine realism | Replace the familiar checker/flame vocabulary with an original route-pulse, meter-tick, or broken-chevron motif. Keep decals large, sparse, and swappable. |
| Visual language | Hot-rod, stunt, street-fashion, and punk energy; cars feel owned by characters rather than a municipality | Combine playful public-service hardware with rebellious personalization. The taxi should look improvised for earning fares at impossible speed, not like a race car merely painted yellow. |

## Modular opportunities for production

Use a stable **core** for floorpan, cabin opening, glass, doors, interior/passenger anchors, wheelbase, damage/collision proxy, and LOD origin. Expose named hardpoints so variants remain compatible.

Highest-value interchangeable groups:

1. **Front clip:** bumper/impact bar, grille, splitter, headlights, fog lights.
2. **Hood and upper nose:** smooth, vented, scoop, exposed utility hardware.
3. **Rear clip:** bumper, taillight panel, diffuser/exhaust, deck vents.
4. **Roof bridge:** sign, meter lights, antenna, luggage/boost accessory; provide a delete/targa version.
5. **Wheel package:** wheel, tire, brake, hub, fender cap, suspension-height preset.
6. **Character kit:** mirrors, side pipes, spoiler, mud flaps, protective rails, taxi meter, interior charm.
7. **Graphic kit:** base color, secondary blocking mask, route motif, door number, fictional sponsors, wear layer.

Practical authoring rule: a swap should change either the silhouette, a major contrast boundary, or a gameplay implication. If it changes none of those, it is low priority. Two core shells are preferable to distorting one topology into every archetype: one compact/targa shell and one four-seat utility shell can share hardpoint conventions, materials, wheels, signs, accessories, and decals.

## Originality guardrails

Avoid reproducing the exact 1960 cruiser body, 1955 jalopy, Hot-D prewar nose, High Roller's rear-deck/lamp arrangement, character license plates, official flame/tire logo, proprietary typography, or the same checker placement. Yellow and generic taxi signage are broad category cues, but their exact combination with a copied silhouette and graphics would collapse the design back toward imitation.

A distinct next-generation direction should pass this test: with all decals hidden and the body rendered as a gray silhouette, it should not be mistaken for any reviewed Crazy Taxi car. With the original taxi kit restored, it should still read as an arcade taxi in under a second. That balances lineage with authorship.
